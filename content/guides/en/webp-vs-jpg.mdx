Sometimes the image you save from a website turns out to be `.webp`, or a speed-test tool tells you to switch to WebP. Both formats store photos in small files, but they are used differently.

## The key differences

| | JPG | WebP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduced | 1992 | 2010 |
| Compression | Lossy | Lossy and lossless |
| Transparent background | No | Yes |
| Animation | No | Yes |
| Where it opens | Nearly every program and service | Mostly modern browsers; some programs don't support it |

JPG has no transparent background and loses a little quality each time you save, but its overwhelming strength is that it opens everywhere. WebP can be smaller at the same quality and supports transparency, but older programs and upload forms sometimes reject it.

## How different is the file size?

Google says WebP is roughly 25-35% smaller than JPG at the same quality. What does it look like in practice? Two photos taken for this guide (a foil-wrapped chicken dish and a dog's face) were saved with this site's converter at the same quality numbers and compared. Both started from a PNG scaled down to 1280px on the long side.

![The two photos used for the test: a foil-wrapped chicken dish on the left and a dog's face on the right, scaled down to 960×1280 and 952×1280](/images/guides/test-photos.jpg)

| Photo | Quality | JPG | WebP | How much smaller WebP is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chicken | 90 | 343KB | 300KB | about 13% |
| Chicken | 80 | 226KB | 171KB | about 24% |
| Chicken | 60 | 146KB | 107KB | about 27% |
| Dog | 90 | 272KB | 218KB | about 20% |
| Dog | 80 | 175KB | 111KB | about 37% |
| Dog | 60 | 109KB | 66.2KB | about 39% |

But **the same quality number is not the same visual quality**, because the two encoders behave differently. Measuring closeness to the source with PSNR (in dB; higher means closer), JPG 80 scores 38.8dB and WebP 80 scores 37.7dB on the dog photo. Part of why WebP came out smaller is that it threw away more.

So the two were compared again **at the same closeness to the source**. Drawing straight lines between the measured points to estimate, at the same PSNR WebP was about 30% smaller than JPG for the chicken photo and about 23% smaller for the dog photo. That is close to the 25-35% Google mentions. This is the result for two photos, and PSNR is only one of several ways to measure quality, so treat the figure as an estimate.

![The same area enlarged 3×: the PNG source, JPG 80 (175KB), WebP 80 (111KB) and HEIC 50 (119KB). They are almost indistinguishable by eye; WebP 80 smooths the fine hairs very slightly more](/images/guides/photo-zoom-jpg-webp-heic.png)

Enlarging the same area (around the dog's eye) shows little difference. WebP 80 smooths the fine hairs only slightly more. (HEIC 50 is covered in the [HEIC guide](/guides/what-is-heic/).)

## When a transparent background matters

Images such as logos and icons that need a transparent background can't be saved as JPG. That leaves PNG and WebP. WebP keeps transparency and is usually smaller than PNG, which suits web graphics. If you must convert a transparent WebP to JPG, the transparent areas are filled with a background color, so pick one that fits where it will be used.

## Recommendations by situation

- **Photos for a website:** WebP. Smaller files make pages load faster. If you must support very old browsers, keep a JPG as a fallback.
- **Email attachments, messenger transfers, certificates and documents:** JPG. It has the lowest risk of the recipient being unable to open it.
- **Uploading to a blog or shop admin screen:** Check the allowed formats first. If WebP is refused, convert to JPG.
- **Originals you'll edit:** Re-saving as JPG or WebP many times piles up degradation, so it's safer to keep the original in a lossless format such as PNG.

<Callout title="A caution when converting">
Converting JPG to WebP, or WebP to JPG, compresses an already lossy image again. Keep the quality at 85-90 or higher and, if possible, convert from the original only once.
</Callout>
